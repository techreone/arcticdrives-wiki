Arctic Drive Repair Guide: Fix What Strands You

A damage-priority guide for Arctic Drive: which broken parts stop the truck, what to salvage, and how to limp home on a bent chassis.

Last Updated: 2026-11-27

Quick answer: Repair in this order: battery, radiator, engine, tires, heat, lights. A truck that starts, cools itself and rolls can reach a workbench; a truck with a perfect paint job and a dead battery cannot. Carry one repair kit before you need it.

The damage model is the reason Arctic Drive is not a point-and-drive truck game. The store page says the chassis bends and parts break off, and the launch patch made doors, mirrors and roof lights harder to knock off while keeping the important bits reachable. That sounds forgiving until a radiator lets go 8 km from the nearest garage. The hauling guide→ explains why a heavy load makes this worse; this page is the repair decision tree.

Which damage actually strands you?

Sort damage by what it takes away, not by how expensive it looks.

System Symptom Priority Field answer
Battery No crank, dead dash Critical Swap in a charged battery from a donor car
Radiator Engine overheats, power drops Critical Replace or patch before the next climb
Engine Knocking, no power, smoke Critical Limp at low load; do not tow heavy
Tire or wheel Pulls, thumps, poor grip High Swap the wheel; keep one spare
Fuel tank Leak or unreachable filler High Reach it from the dented side; patch if the kit allows
Cabin heater No heat, frosted windshield High Fix before night; hypothermia is faster than you think
Doors and mirrors Cold draft, no rear view Medium Fix at the next bench, not on the shoulder
Lights No night visibility Medium Daylight driving or a torch
Cosmetic body Dents, missing trim Low Ignore until the truck is mechanically sound

v0.23.2 made the fuel tank reachable on dented cars. If a prompt is missing, walk around the damaged side before assuming the part is gone.

What should you fix first after a crash?

  1. Stop the engine and get out. A bent chassis can hide a fuel leak, and a hot engine next to a leak is a bad place to inspect.
  2. Check the wheels. A destroyed tire is the cheapest stranded-on-the-road failure to fix.
  3. Open the hood and read the inspection panel. Look for the battery, radiator, oil and the part that is actually damaged.
  4. Replace the critical part from your kit or a donor car. Do not start with cosmetic panels.
  5. Top up oil and coolant before the test drive. A dry engine undoes the repair in one climb.
  6. Drive one slow loop. If temperature climbs or the steering pulls, stop and re-check before committing to a long leg.
Repair in daylight

A shoulder repair at night costs torch battery and patience. If the truck still rolls and the temperature holds, limp to the next shelter and fix it under a light.

How do repair kits and the workbench work?

Repair kits are crafted, not bought from a menu. The launch-window workbench screenshot shows a Good Repair Kit at 100 scrap and an Awesome Repair Kit at 200 scrap, alongside a cargo box at 35 and a flare gun at 60. Treat those numbers as the current tier prices to verify at your own bench, not as universal constants.

A repair kit is consumed per fix, so treat it as a route resource: one per truck, plus a spare if towing. Do not burn your last kit on a door.

The workbench also drives blueprint research. If a repair kit recipe is locked, you need to research it before you can craft it, which is why a sandbox run without a kit can feel like a soft lock. The recycling and crafting guide→ walks through the scrap-to-blueprint loop.

What can you salvage from a donor car?

A abandoned car is a parts store. Use the sledgehammer to remove what you need, then carry it to your truck. Prioritize:

  • Battery — the single most common no-start cause.
  • Wheel and tire — matches the axle, and a full wheel is faster than a bare tire.
  • Radiator — fragile, common, and easy to overlook.
  • Doors and mirrors — comfort and visibility, not survival.
  • Roof lights — useful at night, but only after the truck runs.

How do you limp home with a bent chassis?

Drive slower than you want to. A bent frame changes the steering angle, so the truck may pull under braking and wander on camber. Keep both hands on the wheel, brake early and in a straight line, and avoid any bend that requires a sudden correction.

  • Drop the trailer first if you have one. It amplifies every sway.
  • Move the heaviest cargo off the damaged corner if the suspension is visibly sagging.
  • Run the cabin heater, not the air conditioning; you need heat for the windshield and your body temperature.
  • Take the main road even if it is longer. A shortcut through deep snow is how a bent truck becomes a stuck truck.
  • Stop every few kilometres and look at the wheels and the temperature gauge.

If the truck will not hold coolant or the steering has a dead spot, stop. A repair you can walk away from is better than a rollover you cannot.

When should you abandon the truck?

Abandon when the cost of the next repair is higher than the value of the load and the distance to shelter is walkable. In practice, that means no battery, no radiator, no repair kit and no donor car within a few kilometres. Take the food, the warm clothes and the repair kit, mark the truck on the map, and walk to the nearest cabin.

Do not abandon a truck because it looks bad. Dents and missing mirrors are comfort problems; can it start, cool and steer?

FAQ

Can you fix a tire without a spare?

You need a replacement wheel or tire. A repair kit fixes a damaged part, but it does not create a wheel out of scrap on the shoulder. Carry one spare before you leave the main road.

Does cold weather damage the truck?

Cold affects you more than the truck. The real risks are hypothermia, frosted glass and a dead battery.

Should I repair at a garage or in the field?

Field repairs are for critical parts. Save comfort repairs for a garage.
