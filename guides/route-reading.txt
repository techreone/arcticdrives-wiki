<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> Plan every leg around a fuel stop, a shelter and a bail-out point. Slow before icy bends, never brake mid-corner, and treat 25 km as the maximum road distance, not a safe range. In a whiteout, follow known landmarks and stop rather than guess.</p>
<p>The map is procedural, but the decisions are not. Gas stations, cabins, supermarkets and garages are fixed enough to plan around, and the road keeps throwing the same traps: a crest that hides a bend, a downhill sheet of ice, a gate that looks passable until you hit it at 20 km/h. The <a href="/guides/hauling-and-fuel" class="guide-action-capsule" data-link-type="internal-capsule">hauling guide<span class="capsule-arrow">→</span></a> covers what the load does to your stopping distance; this page is about reading the road before the road reads you.</p>

<h2 id="what-should-you-mark-on-the-map-before-driving">What should you mark on the map before driving?</h2>
<p>Mark four things before you turn the key:</p>
<p>A crew divides map work differently; the <a href="/guides/co-op-roles" class="guide-action-capsule" data-link-type="internal-capsule">co-op roles guide<span class="capsule-arrow">→</span></a> explains who marks the next stop.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>The next fuel stop.</strong> Not the furthest one you can reach; the next one you can reach with a 15 km reserve.</li>
<li><strong>A shelter on the route.</strong> A cabin, garage or stash room you can reach if the weather turns.</li>
<li><strong>A bail-out point.</strong> A wide spot, a station forecourt or a flat shoulder where you can turn around.</li>
<li><strong>The return leg.</strong> A route that works one way may not work when the truck is heavier and the light is gone.</li>
</ol>
<p>If you cannot mark all four, the leg is not planned. It is a gamble with extra steps.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-you-read-a-gas-station-approach">How do you read a gas station approach?</h2>
<p>A station is more than a fuel pump. It is a lit, flat, usually snow-cleared area with a canopy, which makes it the best bad-weather stop on the map. Approach it slowly and look for three things:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The pump or jerrycan stack.</strong> Fuel may be on the shelf, not in the ground tank.</li>
<li><strong>A parked car.</strong> A car with fuel in it can be siphoned, which extends a leg you thought was over.</li>
<li><strong>The exit.</strong> Do not park under the canopy with the truck pointed into a dead end.</li>
</ul>
<p>Stations also attract clutter and sometimes a fence or gate. The v0.23.2 patch made fences, gates and chain locks break when rammed at 15 km/h or more. That is a useful escape hatch, not a driving technique; a gate that breaks may also damage the truck.</p>

<p>The Nisei N100 is four-wheel drive and has better grip than the rear-drive starter trucks, but four driven wheels do not create friction on ice. If the truck is sliding, the answer is less throttle and less steering, not more of both.</p>
<div class="callout callout-warning"><div class="callout-title">The 15 km/h rule cuts both ways</div><div class="callout-body"><p>Gates and fences break at 15 km/h, but so does your front end. If you would not hit a gate at that speed in real life, do not do it with a full cargo load.</p></div></div>
<h2 id="how-do-you-navigate-in-a-whiteout">How do you navigate in a whiteout?</h2>
<p>Whiteout navigation is dead reckoning with a truck. You need a heading, a distance and a confirmed landmark, not a vague sense that the road was here a minute ago.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Slow to walking pace or stop.</strong> The fastest way through a whiteout is usually to wait it out.</li>
<li><strong>Follow the roadside, not the horizon.</strong> Look for poles, fences, tire tracks and guardrails.</li>
<li><strong>Use the map, not the scenery.</strong> The map still shows the road when the world does not.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the heater on and the windows clear.</strong> Frost on the inside is as blinding as snow outside.</li>
<li><strong>If you are lost, stop and save.</strong> A saved position is easier to recover than a roll into a ditch.</li>
</ul>
<p>Airdrops use smoke by day and a strobe at night, and they carry a full jerrycan if your car is running dry. They are a rescue, not a route plan.</p>
<h2 id="what-does-the-map-ui-actually-show">What does the map UI actually show?</h2>
<p>The map shows the road network, your position and marked points of interest. It does not show every obstacle, every parked car or the current snow depth. Treat it as a planning tool, not a live traffic feed.</p>
<p>The map table in a base is better for the big picture: it gives you room to compare routes and mark a return leg. The hand map is for a quick check before a turn. In co-op, the passenger owns the map; the driver owns the road.</p>
<h2 id="what-is-a-safe-route-rule">What is a safe route rule?</h2>
<p>Use the <strong>three-stop rule</strong>: never drive a leg that does not have a fuel stop, a shelter and a turnaround within reach. On a 25 km map, that usually means planning in 8-12 km hops, not one heroic crossing.</p>
<p>At 0.6 km/L, 10 km costs about 17 L before load penalties — most of a jerrycan. A route with two known stations is a route; a route with one maybe-station is a story you tell after walking home.</p>
<h2 id="faq">FAQ</h2>
<h3 id="does-the-map-reset-every-run">Does the map reset every run?</h3>
<p>The road is procedural, so layout and loot can change between runs. Fixed points of interest and the general road structure give you anchors, but do not memorize a single route as universal.</p>
<h3 id="can-you-fast-travel">Can you fast travel?</h3>
<p>No. The truck is the way out, and driving is the game. Plan routes you can actually complete, not routes you hope to skip.</p>
<h3 id="what-is-the-best-landmark-to-navigate-by">What is the best landmark to navigate by?</h3>
<p>A gas station canopy, a wind turbine or a cabin with a light. Pick something visible from a distance and confirm it on the map before you rely on it in a storm.</p>
